# Escape from Jerusalem — Development Journal

## August 14, 2026 — The Game Finally Found Its Core

Today was a pretty significant day for **Escape from Jerusalem**.

I've been circling around the question of what this game should actually *be*. The original prototype was a 2D top-down adventure inspired by Zelda, but the more I've worked on it, the more I've questioned whether combat and traditional adventure mechanics are really the best fit for this story.

Today, I think I found a much more interesting direction.

### The player's inventory is information

The biggest design breakthrough was realizing that the player doesn't necessarily need to collect a bunch of physical items.

Instead, **information can be the inventory.**

The player might know:

* Laban was seen somewhere five minutes ago.
* A merchant knows something about him.
* A guard saw someone matching his description.
* A courier carries messages between Jerusalem and the hills.
* An NPC can be trusted—but another NPC can't.

The player accumulates these pieces of information and uses them to construct a picture of what's happening.

That turns the game into something much closer to a **detective/investigation adventure**.

### NPCs become the game world

This also changed how I'm thinking about NPCs.

Instead of NPCs simply standing in one place waiting for the player to click on them, they should have their own routines, relationships, knowledge, and motivations.

They may have patterns, but their behavior shouldn't necessarily be identical every day.

That means the player can't simply memorize:

> "Laban is always at the stable at 2 PM."

Instead, they might learn:

> "Laban *often* visits the stable in the afternoon."

That's a much more interesting problem.

The player has to investigate the **current situation**, rather than simply solving a static puzzle.

### We actually played the prototype

We tested this concept directly through a conversational game today.

The objective was simple:

> **Find Laban.**

What followed became a surprisingly elaborate investigation.

I searched buildings, waited for information, questioned guards, followed roads, investigated the watchtower, discovered a mysterious courier network, found a boy carrying messages, followed Laban, secretly observed a meeting, rescued the boy, and eventually earned Laban's trust.

The important part wasn't the specific story we improvised.

It was the **game loop that emerged naturally from it**:

> **Explore → Talk → Gather Information → Evaluate Clues → Follow Leads → Build Relationships → Adapt → Find Laban → Earn His Trust**

That actually felt like gameplay.

### Waiting became a mechanic

One particularly interesting discovery was that **waiting can be an action**.

If NPCs have changing routines, waiting isn't just skipping time.

It changes the world.

Someone might leave.

Someone else might arrive.

A piece of information might become outdated.

Laban might move.

The player has to decide:

> **Do I act on what I know now, or wait for more information?**

That's a surprisingly powerful decision for such a simple mechanic.

### Failure became useful

Another thing I liked was that failure didn't necessarily mean restarting.

When the player initially failed to convince Laban to give up the plates, that failure provided information about what Laban wanted.

That suggests a broader philosophy for the game:

> **Failure should usually teach you something.**

Getting the wrong information, angering an NPC, missing someone, or arriving too late should change the player's understanding of the world rather than simply displaying a Game Over screen.

### The new MVP

By the end of the experiment, I had enough confidence to define a new vertical slice.

The new MVP will contain a small section of Jerusalem and the surrounding hills, with roughly ten important NPCs and a handful of locations.

The entire objective is:

> **Find Laban and obtain the plates.**

But the player has to figure out how.

The new prototype will include:

* NPC information networks
* Knowledge/information inventory
* NPC trust and relationships
* Changing NPC locations
* Waiting and time progression
* Contradictory information
* Environmental clues
* Conversational investigation
* A social challenge with Laban

The goal isn't to build the whole game yet.

It's to answer one question:

> **Is conversational investigation actually fun?**

### Preserving the history of the project

I also decided that the game should preserve its own development history.

The new main menu will allow players to see and launch the different versions of *Escape from Jerusalem* we've created so far.

That means the project itself becomes a little archive of its evolution—from the original prototype through the various experiments to this new conversational investigation MVP.

I really like that idea.

Instead of hiding the experiments that didn't work, the game can show how it evolved.

---

## Today's takeaway

I started today thinking about how to build more gameplay into *Escape from Jerusalem*.

I ended the day realizing that **the conversation itself might be the gameplay.**

The player doesn't need to be the strongest person in Jerusalem.

They need to be the person who knows:

**who to ask, what to ask, what to believe, where to look, when to wait, and what to do with what they've learned.**

That's a much more interesting game.

And I think we've finally found the core of **Escape from Jerusalem**.
